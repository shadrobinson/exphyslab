Estimating V̇O2 max without going to max

Read this before lab. It covers what V̇O2 is, why the maximum matters, and how the three submaximal tests we run turn a heart rate into an estimate.

What V̇O2 is

Two people climb the same four flights of stairs at the same pace. At the top, one is chatting and the other is bent over, breathing hard. They did the same work. The difference is how close that work came to the most oxygen each body can take in and use.

V̇O2 is the volume of oxygen your body uses per minute. The dot over the V means "per minute," a rate. Sitting still, you use a small amount. Climbing stairs, your muscles demand more, so you breathe harder, your heart beats faster, and V̇O2 rises to match the work.

Oxygen has to travel a whole chain to be used: lungs load it into blood, the heart pumps that blood, and the working muscle pulls the oxygen out. That chain is summed up in one equation, the Fick equation.

V̇O2 = cardiac output × a-vO2 difference

Cardiac output is how much blood the heart delivers each minute (heart rate × stroke volume). The a-vO2 difference is how much oxygen the muscles take out of each unit of blood, the gap between arterial blood going in and venous blood coming back.1

Breaking down cardiac output

Stand up, walk, then break into a jog. Your heart responds in two ways at once: it beats faster, and it pushes out more blood with each beat.

Cardiac output (Q̇) = heart rate × stroke volume
Typical young adultHeart rateStroke volumeCardiac output
Sitting at rest70 bpm70 mLabout 5 L/min
Maximal exercise195 bpm105 mLabout 20 L/min

The two do not rise together the whole way. In most people stroke volume climbs early and then levels off, at roughly 40 to 60 percent of V̇O2 max. From that point to maximum, the only way to deliver more blood is a faster heart rate.1

This is why we can use heart rate alone. Once stroke volume has leveled off, heart rate tracks cardiac output, and cardiac output tracks V̇O2. Stroke volume is hard to measure in a teaching lab. Heart rate takes two fingers and a clock. It is also why each test below asks for a heart rate above about 110 to 125 bpm: lower than that, stroke volume is still changing and heart rate is a poor stand-in.

Where this breaks downThe plateau is not universal. In endurance-trained athletes, stroke volume often keeps rising all the way to maximum.7, 8 Each added heartbeat then delivers more oxygen than a straight line predicts, so a test that extends the line from submaximal heart rates tends to underestimate athletes.

Two ways to report it

  • Absolute, in L/min. Total oxygen used. Best for non-weight-bearing work like cycling or rowing, and for calculating energy cost.
  • Relative, in mL/kg/min. Divided by body mass. Best for comparing people of different sizes and for weight-bearing work like running.
  • METs. One MET is resting V̇O2, set at 3.5 mL/kg/min. A 10-MET activity uses ten times resting oxygen.

Physiology and energy

Why we care

Muscle runs on ATP. For anything longer than a couple of minutes, almost all of that ATP is made aerobically, in the mitochondria, with oxygen as the final step. So oxygen use is a direct readout of aerobic energy production: for every liter of oxygen consumed, the body releases roughly 5 kcal.1

That link is why one measurement tells us so much. If we know V̇O2, we know how hard the aerobic system is working, how many calories an activity costs, and how the heart, lungs, blood, and muscle are performing together.

Where this breaks downThe 5 kcal per liter figure shifts a little with the fuel mix (about 4.7 for fat, about 5.05 for carbohydrate). And V̇O2 only counts aerobic energy. In a sprint or the first seconds of hard work, phosphocreatine and glycolysis supply energy that oxygen uptake does not show.

The ceiling

What the max tells us

Keep raising the workload and V̇O2 keeps rising, until it stops. That ceiling is V̇O2 max: the highest rate at which a person can take in, deliver, and use oxygen. It is the single best measure of cardiorespiratory fitness.1, 2

  • Health. Low cardiorespiratory fitness is a strong predictor of cardiovascular disease and early death.
  • Performance. It sets the upper limit for endurance events, though it does not decide the winner on its own.
  • Exercise prescription. Training intensities are set as a percentage of it.
  • Tracking change. Retesting shows whether a training program is working.

In a true max test the client exercises to exhaustion while a metabolic cart measures expired air. We accept the result as a real max when we see signs such as a plateau in V̇O2 despite a higher workload, a respiratory exchange ratio of 1.10 or above, heart rate near the age-predicted max, and a very high RPE.1, 2

Where this breaks downMany people stop before showing a clear plateau, so the highest value recorded is often reported as "V̇O2 peak" instead. And two athletes with the same max can perform very differently, because lactate threshold and movement economy also matter.

The practical problem

Why the max test is not always appropriate

A 58-year-old who has not exercised in years signs up at your facility. You want to know their fitness level. Putting them on a treadmill and pushing them to exhaustion on day one is not the right first move.

  • Risk. Maximal effort carries more risk for older, deconditioned, or clinical clients and may call for medical clearance and supervision.
  • Equipment and cost. A metabolic cart is expensive and needs calibration and a trained operator.
  • Time. One person at a time, with setup and recovery.
  • Motivation. The result is only a max if the client truly goes to max. Many will not or cannot.
  • Discomfort. A bad first experience can put a new client off exercise.

Submaximal tests trade some accuracy for safety, speed, and low cost. That trade is usually worth it.2

The shared idea

How a submax test works

During steady aerobic exercise, heart rate and V̇O2 rise together in a nearly straight line. A fitter person does the same workload at a lower heart rate. So if we measure heart rate at a known workload, we can predict where that line would end up at maximum.1, 2

Every test below uses that one idea. They differ in the equipment and in how they get from heart rate to a number.

Assumptions every submax test makes
  • Heart rate reached a true steady state at each workload.
  • The HR to V̇O2 line stays straight all the way to max. It bends in endurance athletes whose stroke volume keeps rising, which is one likely reason they are underestimated.7, 8
  • Max heart rate is what the age formula says. Real people vary by 10 to 12 beats per minute either way.
  • Everyone uses the same oxygen for the same workload.
  • Nothing else is moving heart rate: caffeine, heat, nerves, dehydration, illness, or medications such as beta blockers.
Because of these, expect an individual estimate to be off by roughly 10 to 15 percent. Treat the number as a range and use it to track change in the same person over time.1, 2

Test 1 · cycle ergometer · single stage

Åstrand-Rhyming

One workload, six minutes. The goal is a steady heart rate between 125 and 170 bpm.3, 2

  1. Set the seat so the knee is slightly bent at the bottom of the pedal stroke. Client pedals at 50 rpm.
  2. Choose a workload. Common starting points: 300 to 600 kgm/min for unconditioned clients, 600 to 900 for conditioned clients, with the lower end for women and the higher end for men.
  3. Ride for 6 minutes. Record heart rate at the end of minute 5 and minute 6.
  4. If the two heart rates are within 5 bpm of each other and between 125 and 170, average them. That is your steady-state HR.
  5. If they differ by more than 5 bpm, keep riding one minute at a time until they agree. If HR is under 125 after 6 minutes, raise the workload and ride another 6 minutes.
  6. Read V̇O2 max from the nomogram (or use the equation below), then multiply by the age correction factor.4

Where this breaks downThe whole estimate rests on a single heart rate, so anything that nudges HR that day moves the result. The nomogram was built on young, fairly fit adults, which is why older clients need the age correction.

Test 2 · cycle ergometer · multistage

YMCA bike protocol

Two to four 3-minute stages. Instead of relying on population averages, this test plots the client's own heart rate line and extends it.5, 2

  1. Client pedals at 50 rpm. Stage 1 is always 0.5 kp (150 kgm/min) for 3 minutes.
  2. Record HR in the last 15 to 30 seconds of minutes 2 and 3. If they differ by more than 5 bpm, add a minute.
  3. Use the stage 1 heart rate to set stage 2 (table below). Each later stage adds 0.5 kp.
  4. Continue until you have two stages with steady HR between 110 bpm and 85 percent of age-predicted max.
  5. Plot those two points, HR against workload. Extend the line to age-predicted max HR (220 − age) and read the workload there.
  6. Convert that max workload to V̇O2 with the ACSM cycling equation.2

Test 3 · step bench · recovery heart rate

McArdle (Queens College) step test

Three minutes of stepping, then one heart rate count. No bike, and a whole class can test at once. Here a fitter person shows a lower heart rate right after the same work.6, 1

  1. Use a 16.25 inch (41.3 cm) step.
  2. Set the metronome: 96 beats per minute for men (24 steps per minute), 88 for women (22 steps per minute). Each step cycle is four beats: up, up, down, down.
  3. Step for exactly 3 minutes.
  4. Stop and stay standing. Starting 5 seconds after the last step, count the pulse for 15 seconds (from second 5 to second 20).
  5. Multiply the count by 4 to get recovery HR in bpm, then use the equation.

Where this breaks downHeart rate drops fast after exercise, so starting the count a few seconds late gives a lower HR and a falsely high estimate. One miscounted beat becomes 4 bpm. A fixed step height is harder for shorter people, and the equations came from college-age students, so they fit other ages less well.

Option 4 · the one already on your wrist

Smart watches

Most fitness watches report a V̇O2 max or "cardio fitness" number. They use the same submax idea: the watch compares your heart rate to your pace during outdoor walks or runs with GPS. Holding a faster pace at a lower heart rate reads as higher fitness.

  • Useful for: trends over weeks and months in the same person, with no test session at all.
  • Less useful for: a single precise value, or comparing two people wearing different brands.
Where this breaks down
  • Wrist optical sensors misread heart rate with a loose band, arm motion, cold skin, or tattoos.
  • Most watches estimate max HR from age unless you enter a measured value.
  • Hills, wind, heat, trails, and treadmill use all distort the pace-to-HR comparison.
  • The algorithms are proprietary, so you cannot check the math the way you can in the three tests above.
  • Validation studies generally find watches lean toward overestimating less fit users and underestimating very fit ones.
